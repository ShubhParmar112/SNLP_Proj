P: 2.  Institutional Context
P: Universities collect textual feedback on teaching, course content, pace, assessment, workload and resources, and then reduce it to numerical averages. The average is actionable only if a coordinator already knows what drove it, which is precisely what the average removes.
P: The problem is not that sentiment analysis is hard. It is that document-level sentiment is the wrong output shape for this data. Consider: “The professor explains concepts clearly, but the assignments take far too much time.” There is no correct single label. Forcing one discards half the information, and the half discarded is arbitrary. Aspect-based sentiment analysis produces teaching:positive and workload:negative, which is what a coordinator can act on.
P: This is why the project scope stops at ABSA rather than extending to summarisation and a dashboard. The brief asks for one strong NLP capability; building seven shallow ones is the most common way projects of this type lose marks on technical depth.

P: 4.  NLP Formulation
P: The work is decomposed into three tasks with distinct targets and metrics. Stating them separately matters: the original framing bundled aspect extraction and sentiment classification into one undifferentiated task, which makes it impossible to tell whether a failure is a missed aspect or a misjudged polarity — two problems with entirely different fixes.
--- TABLE (4 rows x 4 cols) ---
  R0: Task || Target || Type || Primary metric
  R1: A — Document sentiment || One label per comment || 3-class single-label || Macro-F1
  R2: B — Aspect detection || Set of aspect categories || 8-class multi-label || Micro-F1
  R3: C — ABSA (core) || Set of (aspect, polarity) pairs || Structured prediction || Exact-match micro tuple-F1
--- END TABLE ---
P: Task C is what the project is judged on. A predicted tuple counts as correct only when both the aspect category and its polarity match a gold tuple for the same comment. Reporting aspect F1 and polarity accuracy separately — as the original plan did — overstates performance, because neither number penalises finding the right aspect and assigning it the wrong sentiment.
P: Worked example of the metric
P: Gold:      {(teaching, positive), (pace, negative)}
P: Predicted: {(teaching, positive), (pace, neutral), (workload, negative)}
P: TP = 1   FP = 2   FN = 1
P: Precision = 0.333   Recall = 0.500   F1 = 0.400
P: A secondary figure, polarity accuracy restricted to correctly detected aspects, is reported alongside. It separates “found the wrong aspect” from “found the aspect, misjudged the sentiment”, which the headline F1 conflates.
P: 5.  Dataset
P: 5.1  The dataset decision and why it was changed
P: The initial plan was to use a team-authored synthetic corpus. That approach was rejected. If the same six people write the comments and assign the labels, the model learns the team's own phrasing templates. A TF-IDF classifier will score very highly on such data, and the score will measure nothing except internal consistency. It would also sit in direct contradiction with the brief's requirement for genuine quantitative evaluation.
--- TABLE (5 rows x 3 cols) ---
  R0: Corpus || Role || Rationale
  R1: Public course-review dataset || Primary training and evaluation || Real student language: misspellings, code-switching, sarcasm, and a realistic class distribution
  R2: SemEval-2014 Task 4 (ABSA) || Cross-domain benchmark || Gold ABSA annotations; lets us compare against published numbers and run a transfer experiment
  R3: Institutional feedback || Used only with authorisation || Highest domain fit; requires documented permission and anonymisation
  R4: Team-authored synthetic || Fixtures and edge-case supplement only || Never the primary corpus; used to verify the pipeline runs and to cover rare aspects
--- END TABLE ---
P: Training on restaurant/laptop ABSA data and testing on course reviews is itself a defensible cross-domain transfer experiment, and a more interesting result than a high score on self-authored text.

P: 6.  Aspect Taxonomy
P: The taxonomy was reduced from 17 labels to 8. On a corpus of a few hundred comments, 17 categories leave several with fewer than ten examples — too few to train on, too few to evaluate on, and enough to make macro-F1 swing wildly on the basis of two or three test items. A smaller taxonomy that is reliably annotated is worth more than a fine-grained one that is not.
--- TABLE (9 rows x 3 cols) ---
  R0: Label || Covers || Does not cover
  R1: teaching || Explanation clarity, method, examples, lecture delivery || Personal availability → faculty_interaction
  R2: faculty_interaction || Approachability, doubt resolution, responsiveness || Quality of explanation → teaching
  R3: course_content || Syllabus relevance, depth, structure, intrinsic difficulty || Delivery speed → pace
  R4: pace || Speed of delivery, rushing, coverage vs time || Out-of-class work volume → workload
  R5: assessment || Assignments, exams, quizzes, projects, grading fairness || Volume of assessment → workload
  R6: workload || Quantity of work, deadline pressure, clashes || Difficulty of one task → assessment
  R7: resources || Slides, notes, recordings, LMS, lab, classroom, equipment || —
  R8: other || Clear evaluative statement outside the seven above || Non-evaluative text → no aspect at all
--- END TABLE ---
P: other is a holding label, not a dustbin. Every use records the triggering phrase. If a theme appears five or more times, it is promoted to a real category, the guidelines version is bumped and affected rows are re-labelled.
P: 7.  Annotation Scheme
P: Three levels per comment: document sentiment (L1), aspect categories present (L2), and polarity plus supporting character span for each aspect (L3). L3 is the deliverable; L1 exists only to demonstrate the limitation it addresses.
P: 7.1  Polarity labels and the distinction that was being blurred
P: The earlier draft instructed annotators to use neutral whenever sentiment was unclear, while separately showing a mixed/neutral label in an example. Those are three different situations collapsed into one label, which turns neutral into a garbage class and makes macro-F1 uninterpretable. They are now separated:
--- TABLE (4 rows x 2 cols) ---
  R0: Situation || Handling
  R1: Aspect mentioned, no evaluation given || polarity = neutral
  R2: Both polarities about the same aspect || polarity = conflict
  R3: Annotator cannot decide || best guess + uncertain = 1 (metadata, never a training class)
--- END TABLE ---
P: The four-way set (positive / negative / neutral / conflict) is taken from SemEval-2014 Task 4 so that our results remain comparable with published ABSA work rather than using a bespoke scheme.
P: 7.2  Spans
P: Every aspect–polarity pair records the shortest supporting span as character offsets into the original text. This is mandatory from Week 1 because the Week 3–4 transformer ABSA model requires token-level supervision; retrofitting spans later would mean re-annotating the entire corpus. The loader enforces feedback_text[start:end] == aspect_term and rejects the file otherwise, which catches offsets drifting after a spreadsheet edit — the most common annotation error in practice.
P: 7.3  Workflow
P: Round 0 — Calibration. All six members annotate the same 40 comments. Agreement is computed. Production annotation does not begin until κ ≥ 0.60.
P: Round 1 — Production. Three pairs work on disjoint slices. Each pair double-annotates 20% of its slice and splits the remainder.
P: Round 2 — Adjudication. Disagreements and all uncertain = 1 rows go to a third member outside the pair. Every decision is logged in docs/adjudication_log.md with the rule that settled it.
P: 8.  Inter-Annotator Agreement
P: With six annotators and a hand-built corpus, IAA is the only evidence that the gold labels mean the same thing across the dataset. It was absent from the original plan; it is now a gate rather than a footnote. Computed by src/iaa.py on the double-annotated subset.
--- TABLE (6 rows x 4 cols) ---
  R0: Level || Metric || Target || Result
  R1: Aspect detection || Per-category Cohen's κ, averaged || ≥ 0.60 || [TO FILL]
  R2: Aspect detection || Pairwise micro-F1 between annotators || — || [TO FILL]
  R3: Polarity (shared aspects) || Cohen's κ over 4 labels || ≥ 0.60 || [TO FILL]
  R4: Span boundaries || Proportion with Jaccard ≥ 0.5 || — || [TO FILL]
  R5: Corpus difficulty || Proportion of rows with uncertain = 1 || — || [TO FILL]
--- END TABLE ---
P: Interpretation follows Landis and Koch: below 0.40 poor, 0.40–0.60 moderate, 0.60–0.80 substantial, above 0.80 almost perfect. Per-category κ is reported in full, because the categories with the lowest agreement identify exactly where the guidelines need sharper boundary rules — typically the teaching/pace and assessment/workload pairs.
P: A moderate κ that is reported and diagnosed is worth more than a high κ with no explanation, and far more than no κ at all. If the calibration round fails the 0.60 gate, that fact and the resulting guideline revision are reported as part of the methodology.
P: 9.  Preprocessing
P: Two separate branches, which is a correction to the original single-pipeline design. Feeding lemmatised, stopword-stripped, lowercased text to a pretrained transformer destroys the casing, punctuation and function words that such a model depends on — a reliable way to make the Week 4 “improved” model underperform the baseline for reasons unrelated to the model itself.
--- TABLE (9 rows x 3 cols) ---
  R0: Step || Branch A — classical (TF-IDF) || Branch B — transformer
  R1: PII redaction || Yes || Yes
  R2: Whitespace normalisation || Yes || Yes
  R3: Lowercasing || Yes || No — casing is signal
  R4: Punctuation removal || Yes || No
  R5: Contraction expansion || Yes || No
  R6: Negation scope marking || Yes — NOT_ prefix to clause boundary || No — attention handles it
  R7: Stopword removal || Selective — negation and contrast words retained || No
  R8: Lemmatisation || Optional, ablated || No — destroys subword morphology
--- END TABLE ---
P: 9.1  Negation handling
P: A general stopword list deletes not, never, but and however — the exact tokens that carry sentiment and contrast in feedback text. Our list explicitly retains them. In addition, tokens inside a negation's scope are prefixed so that a bag-of-words model can distinguish them:
P: "the lectures were not very informative"
P:   →  ... not  NOT_very  NOT_informative
P: Scope terminates at a clause boundary rather than a fixed window, so “not clear but the notes are good” does not wrongly negate good. Its contribution is measured as an ablation, not assumed: [TO FILL — Δ micro tuple-F1 with negation marking on vs off].
P: The original feedback text is retained unmodified alongside both processed forms, because all span offsets and all API output refer to it.

P: 11.  Split and Leakage Controls
P: The aspect target is multi-label, so train_test_split(stratify=y) does not apply. We use iterative stratification (Sechidis, Tsoumakas and Vlahavas, 2011), which allocates examples so that each label's distribution is preserved across splits, with near-duplicate groups kept intact.
--- TABLE (7 rows x 2 cols) ---
  R0: Control || Implementation
  R1: Fixed seed || RANDOM_SEED = 42 in src/config.py, used by every stochastic step
  R2: Frozen split || Computed once into data/processed/splits.json and committed; never recomputed
  R3: Vectoriser fitting || Every transformation inside an sklearn Pipeline, so it can only see the training fold
  R4: Duplicate leakage || Near-duplicate groups assigned wholesale to one split
  R5: Test-set discipline || Test opened once at end of Week 2 and once at end of Week 4; no decision is made on it
  R6: Lexicon circularity || Aspect lexicon built from the training split only, by members not annotating validation or test; per-term provenance logged
--- END TABLE ---

P: 12.  Baseline Implementation
--- TABLE (5 rows x 2 cols) ---
  R0: Model || Method
  R1: A — Document sentiment || TF-IDF (word 1–2 gram + char 3–5 gram) → Logistic Regression, class_weight='balanced'
  R2: B — Aspect detection || Same features → one-vs-rest Logistic Regression over 8 categories
  R3: B′ — Lexicon aspect detection || Transparent keyword/phrase lexicon, seeded from domain knowledge and expanded from training-split statistics
  R4: C — ABSA || Stage 1: aspect detection. Stage 2: a single shared polarity classifier over (comment, aspect) pairs with the aspect injected as an explicit token
--- END TABLE ---
P: The Stage 2 design matters on a small corpus. Eight separate per-aspect polarity classifiers would each see too few examples to learn anything; one shared model conditioned on the aspect identity pools the signal across categories while still producing aspect-specific predictions.
P: Character n-grams are included deliberately: student feedback contains misspellings (assignmnet), inconsistent spacing and code-switching, all of which produce out-of-vocabulary word tokens but remain matchable at the character level.
P: Logistic regression is chosen for the baseline because it is interpretable and fast, not because it is expected to be strong. Its coefficients can be inspected per aspect, which makes the error analysis in Section 15 concrete rather than speculative.
P: 13.  Evaluation Methodology
P: Fixed before any result was produced, so that the Week 4 comparison is a test rather than a search for a flattering number.
P: 13.1  Mandatory reference baselines
P: Every model figure is reported in the same table as a majority-class and a stratified-random reference. Without them no accuracy figure can be interpreted — an accuracy of 0.78 is a strong result if the majority class is 0.41 and a failure if it is 0.81. The original plan's results table had no such row.
P: 13.2  Uncertainty
P: Development figures: 5-fold cross-validation on train + validation, reported as mean ± standard deviation.
P: Test figures: 95% bootstrap confidence interval over 1000 resamples, reported as F1 = 0.71 [0.63, 0.78].
P: A baseline-vs-improved difference whose intervals overlap substantially is reported as inconclusive, not as an improvement.
P: At a corpus of a few hundred comments, a 15% test split is a few dozen items. A single point estimate on that many examples is too noisy to support a comparison claim, and presenting one as if it were settled is the most likely way this project would report a result that does not replicate.
P: 13.3  Low-support categories
P: Any aspect category with fewer than 20 test instances is reported but marked low-support and excluded from the headline macro average. An F1 of 0.00 or 1.00 computed over three examples is not a finding.

P: 16.1  Score semantics
P: The original specification returned "confidence": 0.91 for lexicon-derived aspects. That number was invented: a keyword either matched or it did not, and a match has no probability attached. Separately, predict_proba output is not calibrated — 0.9 does not mean “correct 90% of the time”. Every score therefore carries an explicit type:
--- TABLE (4 rows x 3 cols) ---
  R0: score_type || Meaning || Safe to threshold?
  R1: lexicon_match_count || Count of distinct lexicon terms matched || Ranking only
  R2: uncalibrated_probability || Raw predict_proba; monotone, not a frequency || Yes, with a locally tuned threshold
  R3: calibrated_probability || After Platt/isotonic calibration on held-out data || Yes
--- END TABLE ---
P: Weeks 1–2 emit only the first two. Calibration is a Week 5 deliverable.
P: 16.2  Group 6 integration
P: Group 5 emits course-level advisory signals carrying explicit advisory_only and not_for_individual_action flags. The component performs no individual-level inference and must never be joined to a student identifier. This is stated in the API contract rather than deferred to a Responsible AI section, because a constraint that lives only in a report is not a constraint.

P: 19.  Limitations and Threats to Validity
P: Stated before results exist, so they cannot be selected after the fact to suit whatever the numbers turn out to be.
P: Corpus size. A test split of a few dozen comments yields wide confidence intervals. Comparative claims are therefore reported with intervals and cross-validation, and are treated as suggestive where the intervals overlap.
P: Lexicon circularity. Controls are in place (training-split-only construction, separated annotators, logged provenance), but the risk cannot be eliminated entirely while the same team writes both the lexicon and the gold labels.
P: Annotator pool. Six annotators from one cohort share assumptions about what these comments mean. κ measures consistency within that pool, not agreement with the wider student population.
P: Domain and language. English academic feedback only. Code-switched text is accepted but its performance is untested and separately flagged.
P: Rare labels. conflict and neutral are expected to be scarce. Per-class figures for them will be unstable and are reported as low-support rather than averaged into headline results.
P: Baseline ceiling. Bag-of-words features cannot represent negation scope, sarcasm or implicit aspects. This is a known and expected limitation; it is the specific gap the Week 3–4 transformer is intended to close, and the error analysis is structured to test whether it actually does.
